# Governments and market laws

## The business cycle

Output runs above or below its trend with momentum. Good times and cheap money build up credit, and the more credit, the likelier a bust that leaves borrowers paying debt down for months. Inflation rises when the economy runs hot. Household demand, loan rates, wages and import prices all follow the cycle.

## The central bank

The central bank meets every six weeks and moves its rate in quarter points toward its inflation target. Its chair, one of the game's named characters, leans dovish or hawkish and that tilts every decision. At the end of a four-year term the government reappoints the chair or names a new one. A prime lending rate and a ten-year bond yield follow from the policy rate.

## Government and elections

Two parties compete in an election every four years, decided by the economy. A new government's programme takes effect a quarter after it wins: corporate tax, a low-skill wage floor, the market share at which a merger is reviewed, and union law.

## Market laws

The party in power also sets market laws.

- **Workers' Party:** common carrier rules (owned pipelines, rail and ships sell spare capacity at a capped rate and may not lock a lane up with contracts), review of deals that buy a supplier or customer once their market share reaches 35 percent, a strategic reserve that buys oil, ore and other raw goods in busts and sells in spikes, and nationalisation of failing strategic firms.
- **Merger review:** a new Commerce Party government reviews deals once a firm's share of a market reaches 40 percent; a Workers' Party government, 30 percent.
- **Commerce Party:** production quotas in extraction states (output held to 80 percent of capacity while a price is below 92 percent of its reference level), bailout loans for failing strategic firms, and privatisation of state firms once they earn.

State firms price at cost plus five percent. State rescues are rare: only large, operating-profitable strategic firms get one.
