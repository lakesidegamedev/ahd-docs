# Rivals

Every scenario starts with dozens of rival firms already in the market. They follow the same rules, prices and information as you, and they build, price, borrow, ship and fight the same way.

## Chief executives and temperaments

Rival firms are run by named chief executives drawn from a cast of fictional people, each with a portrait, an age, a home town, a background and a temperament. The temperament decides how the firm plays:

- **Integrators** buy up their suppliers and build their own stores.
- **Raiders** raid, take freight lanes and spend more on marketing.
- **Financiers** bid for companies, float subsidiaries and borrow.
- **Speculators** stockpile goods ahead of price swings.
- **Builders** build.
- **Cautious** firms keep a bigger cash cushion and rarely start fights.

A rival that loses money for six four-week periods in a row can replace its chief executive with someone of a different approach. Difficulty changes only how sharp and how aggressive rivals are.

## Grudges and allies

A rival you raid, bid for or squeeze out of a freight lane holds a **grudge** and comes after you. Grudges fade over about a year. Joint venture partners and the two sides of a friendly deal become **allies**, who are never raided or bid for. A rival's dossier, on its stock page or a region board, lists its grudges, its friends and its recent moves.

## Raids

A raid is a marketing campaign against a rival's division in a market you both serve, to take some of its plants. Every rival row has a Raid button that opens a quote: the chance of success, the plants at stake, the price, the rival's defence, any repeat-raid surcharge and a budget slider. A bigger stake is harder to win, and each repeat raid on the same rival costs more. When a raid is not open, the panel says why.

Rivals raid you too. New companies and newly opened divisions cannot be raided for their first 26 weeks.

## Takeovers

Rivals with the right temperament bid for companies, including yours, and you can bid for theirs. Competition review applies once a deal would give a group too large a share of a market.
