# Your holding company

You play an investor. Your money and your shares sit in a portfolio above your companies, and the companies you control form a group: a holding company with as many subsidiaries as you can build or buy. Losing a company does not end your career.

## Companies, divisions and plants

A company opens a **division** the first time it builds a plant of a sector type in a region. Opening a division costs an entry fee, and the founding plant is built at a discount. Later plants in that region and sector join the division and run its strategy. Retooling moves the whole division to another strategy of the same sector.

Expanding past half of a market costs more, up to three times as much, softened when there are few rivals. Running many divisions adds an overhead that grows smoothly with the division count; owning logistics divisions takes part of it off. Past a firm's construction capacity, building costs more and takes longer.

Each division has a **reputation**, built by quality spending and marketing and lost slowly. It earns a price premium and loyal customers in every market. Raids cost reputation as well as plants.

## Control and the board

More than half of the votes gives a company control of another. Exactly half is not control. A board of five seats is elected by share of the vote, and the board approves large borrowing, large capital spending, mergers and big share issues. Each quarter the board of your primary company sets one goal; meeting it pays you a management bonus.

## Subsidiaries and listings

- **Carve out** a division into a new subsidiary.
- **List** part of a subsidiary on the exchange and keep control.
- **Spin off** a subsidiary to the parent's shareholders.
- **Issue shares**, buy them back or pay dividends, within the board's limits and a cash buffer.

Every operating company trades on the stock exchange at a price that follows its fair value. You can buy and sell shares in any of them, including your rivals. Companies far from their peak, missing board goals, cutting a dividend or squeezing minority holders attract **activist investors**. You can concede, negotiate or fight; if you do nothing the board answers after four weeks.

## Group supply and transfer prices

Companies in one group supply each other first: the engine keeps standing contracts between divisions that use what another makes, ahead of any open-market sale. Each supplying division has one transfer price setting, from its own cost through the market price (the default) to a markup. Pricing under market in a part-owned company takes value from its minority shareholders and can draw a challenge, and profit moves between the tax bills of different states.

## Joint ventures

Two groups can co-own a division as a joint venture with stated stakes and split its profit. Either side can buy out the other or leave after 26 weeks. Joint venture partners become allies: allies are never raided or bid for and are the first asked to share a project.

## Money

- **Loans** from banks priced by your credit.
- **Corporate bonds** priced off the central bank's ten-year yield plus a credit spread from leverage, interest coverage and size. Buy and sell them on the bond list at the market price.
- **Pricing.** Every good can be priced automatically from sell-through, set against the market, or set at a markup over your own cost.
